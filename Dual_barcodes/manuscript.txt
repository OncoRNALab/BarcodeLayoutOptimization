Light-directed photolithographic DNA synthesis enables the fabrication of ultra–highdensity
capture arrays but is intrinsically prone to synthesis errors. A major source
of error arises from UV light scattering during deprotection cycles, which can partially
remove the protective groups of neighboring features. As a consequence, nucleotides
may be incorrectly incorporated at adjacent spots, leading predominantly to insertion
errors. These effects become increasingly relevant as feature size decreases and interspot
distances shrink. Therefore, in addition to barcode sequence design, the spatial
arrangement of barcodes on the array surface represents a critical factor influencing overall
synthesis fidelity.
To mitigate this effect, I explored layout optimization strategies inspired by the work
of Jatzkowski et al. [50], who formalized the problem as a combinatorial optimization task.
In their framework, each barcode is represented by a binary synthesis schedule, where each
bit corresponds to a photolithographic synthesis cycle and takes value 1 if the barcode is
deprotected (i.e., synthesized) during that cycle. The objective is to minimize the number
of instances in which a barcode undergoes synthesis while a neighboring barcode does not,
as such differences increase the likelihood of cross-feature deprotection due to scattered
UV light.
The authors demonstrated that the resulting optimization problem is MaxSNP-hard;
informally, it is as hard as the hardest problems in the MaxSNP class under standard
approximation-preserving reductions. Unless P=NP, MaxSNP-hard problems admit no
polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS), so no efficient algorithm can guarantee
an arbitrarily close approximation to the optimum. This motivates the use of heuristic
approaches. Their proposed solution consists of a greedy placement algorithm followed
by a local-search refinement step. The greedy algorithm fills the array column by column.
For each position, the eight neighboring features are identified and the Hamming distance
between the synthesis schedule of each candidate barcode and those of the already assigned
neighbors is computed. The barcode that minimizes the summed Hamming distance to
its neighbors is selected for that position. To efficiently evaluate all remaining barcode
candidates at each step, GPU acceleration is employed. Subsequently, a local-search
procedure (2-OPT) iteratively swaps barcode pairs if the exchange reduces the global
layout cost, defined as the sum of Hamming distances between synthesis schedules over
all pairs of spatially adjacent features on the array.
Implementation and evaluation. In this project, the greedy algorithm followed by
2-OPT refinement was implemented and adapted to our barcode design framework. In
all of our layout evaluations reported below, spatial barcodes were 36 nt total length.
The starting barcode set was generated as described in Section 2.3.1 (Chapter 2, Computational
challenges: Spatial barcode design for high-error-rate arrays): random 36-nt
candidate sequences, drawn from an enlarged pool and retained after the filtering criteria
stated there. Several modifications were introduced to ensure consistent and comparable
synthesis schedule representations.
First, a normalized synthesis encoding scheme was defined. In practice, naive synthesis
schedules may differ in length depending on nucleotide composition, preventing direct
Hamming distance comparison. To overcome this limitation, each barcode of length N
was encoded using a fixed-length binary vector of 4×N bits. For each nucleotide position,
a 4-bit one-hot representation was used (A, C, G, T), with exactly one bit set to 1. For
example:
ACTG -> 1000 0100 0001 0010
AATG -> 1000 1000 0001 0010
This encoding ensures uniform schedule length and enables consistent Hamming distance
computation across all barcodes.
Second, layout quality was quantified by computing the mean Hamming distance between
each barcode and its eight immediate neighbors. This metric was evaluated for
three configurations: (i) a random layout (baseline), (ii) after greedy optimization, and
(iii) after greedy + 2-OPT refinement. This allowed direct measurement of cost reduction
achieved by each optimization stage.
Third, to assess whether similarity in synthesis schedules translates to sequence-level
similarity, I additionally computed the Levenshtein distance between neighboring barcode
sequences (rather than their synthesis encodings). This analysis provided insight into the
relationship between photolithographic schedule similarity and oligonucleotide sequence
similarity.
Finally, spatial heatmaps were generated to visualize the mean Hamming and Levenshtein
distances of each feature relative to its eight neighbors before and after optimization.
These visualizations revealed the emergence of local clusters of barcodes with
reduced schedule dissimilarity, consistent with the intended objective of minimizing crossfeature
synthesis discrepancies
Co-design of barcodes and layout. In contrast to post-hoc layout optimization of
pre-designed barcode libraries, I explored alternative strategies that jointly design barcode
sequences and spatial arrangements from first principles. These co-design approaches explicitly
embed spatial information into the barcode structure itself, ensuring that sequence
similarity correlates with physical proximity on the array. Three distinct algorithmic
frameworks were developed and evaluated in silico.
Dual barcodes (combinatorial clustering). This method partitions the array into
regular spatial blocks (e.g., 4 × 4 spots) and encodes position using two 18-nt barcode
components, concatenated to yield a 36-nt full barcode. The first component (bar_1 )
serves as a block identifier, shared by all spots within the same spatial block. The second
component (bar_2 ) provides a unique position identifier within each block. Both
components are chosen from fixed, pre-computed 18-nt candidate libraries (sequences are
not designed de novo during layout generation); assignments enforce global uniqueness
of the concatenated barcode (bar_1 + bar_2 ). This architecture implies that all spots
within a spatial block share the same bar_1 sequence, creating spatial clustering tolerant
to synthesis errors affecting the block-level identifier. For a 118 × 182 layout (21,476
features), each of the two input 18-nt libraries contained 21,476 candidate sequences, generated
with the same random sampling and filtering procedure as the greedy barcode pool
(Section 2.3.1), but using 18-nt segments for each component rather than a single 36-nt
barcode.
Quadtree (hierarchical position encoding). Inspired by spatial indexing structures,
this method encodes the (row, column) coordinate of each spot through recursive
subdivision of the array space. Each barcode position l corresponds to one level of the
hierarchy: the coordinate is scaled to a binary space of 2L bins, and at each cycle, the
most significant unprocessed bit from both row and column coordinates defines a quadrant
index (0–3), which is mapped to a nucleotide (A, C, G, T). To prevent synthesis errors
arising from homopolymer runs, a run-length limiting (RLL) algorithm is applied. For
each cycle, a contingency table is constructed to count how many spots at risk of violating
the maximum run constraint would be affected by each of the 24 possible permutations
of the quadrant-to-nucleotide mapping. The permutation that minimizes violations is
selected, and deterministic fallback rules enforce diversity when necessary. This encoding
intrinsically ensures that Hamming distance between barcodes scales logarithmically with
geometric distance on the array. A 36-nucleotide design was implemented using three
rounds of 8 cycles plus 12 random nucleotides, evaluated on a 118 × 182 array (21,476
features).
Zipcodes (Gray code spatial gradients). Gray codes are binary sequences in
which successive integers differ by exactly one bit. By independently applying Gray code
encoding to row and column coordinates and translating each 2-bit pair into a single
nucleotide (00 → A, 01 → C, 10 → G, 11 → T), this method guarantees that spatially
adjacent spots differ by exactly one base in their position-encoding sequences. The final
barcode is constructed by concatenating the row-encoded segment, an optional intersegment
separator (e.g., “GGGG”), the column-encoded segment, and a random highdiversity
sequence for sequencing discrimination. This architecture provides the smoothest
spatial gradient among the tested methods: Hamming distance increases proportionally
with Manhattan distance along the array axes. This design was evaluated on the same
118 × 182 layout (21,476 features).
To illustrate the distinct spatial synthesis patterns generated by each co-design strategy,
Figure 6.3 shows the activation masks for a single representative photolithographic
cycle across the different layout approaches. In random layout, deprotection events are
uniformly distributed across the array surface. In contrast, the greedy-optimized layout
exhibits small localized clusters of co-activated features, reflecting the algorithm’s tendency
to group barcodes with similar synthesis schedules. The three co-design methods
display their characteristic spatial structures: Dual barcodes produce compact 4×4 blocks
corresponding to the spatial block size; Quadtree generates 16 large quadrants reflecting
hierarchical subdivision of the array; and Zipcodes create vertical column-wise activation
patterns resulting from the independent Gray coding of row and column coordinates.
These activation patterns directly influence the susceptibility of each layout to UV light
scattering and cross-feature contamination during synthesis.
The implementation details of these co-design strategies are available in the associated
repository (https://github.com/OncoRNALab/Layout_OPT).
Future perspectives. All barcode designs and layout optimization strategies described
above were generated and evaluated exclusively through computational simulation. Empirical
validation through physical array fabrication and sequencing remains an essential
next step. Future work will involve the photolithographic synthesis of arrays implementing
each of the tested layout strategies—random layout (baseline), greedy + 2-OPT optimization,
Dual barcodes, Quadtree, and Zipcodes—followed by sequencing-based characterization
under controlled conditions. This will enable direct measurement of synthesis
error rates, cross-feature contamination, and decoding performance in real experimental
settings. Such empirical data will be critical for validating the predictive accuracy of
the simulation framework and for identifying conditions under which co-design strategies
provide measurable improvements in array quality and sequencing fidelity. Additionally,
these experiments will inform refinements to both algorithmic design and photolithographic
synthesis protocols, ultimately guiding the selection of optimal barcode-layout
combinations for production-scale spatial transcriptomics arrays.
