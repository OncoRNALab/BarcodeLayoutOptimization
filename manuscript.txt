\subsection{Barcode layout optimization for photolithographic arrays}

Light-directed photolithographic DNA synthesis enables the fabrication of ultra–high-density
capture arrays but is intrinsically prone to synthesis errors. A major source of error arises
from UV light scattering during deprotection cycles, which can partially remove the
protective groups of neighboring features. As a consequence, nucleotides may be
incorrectly incorporated at adjacent spots, leading predominantly to insertion errors.
These effects become increasingly relevant as feature size decreases and inter-spot
distances shrink. Therefore, in addition to barcode sequence design, the spatial
arrangement of barcodes on the array surface represents a critical factor influencing
overall synthesis fidelity.

To mitigate this effect, we explored layout optimization strategies inspired by the work
of B\"ohm et al.~\cite{boehm2024barlay}, who formalized the problem as a combinatorial
optimization task. In their framework, each barcode is represented by a binary synthesis
schedule, where each bit corresponds to a photolithographic synthesis cycle and takes
value 1 if the barcode is deprotected (i.e., synthesized) during that cycle. The objective
is to minimize the number of instances in which a barcode undergoes synthesis while a
neighboring barcode does not, as such differences increase the likelihood of cross-feature
deprotection due to scattered UV light.

The authors demonstrated that the resulting optimization problem is MaxSNP-hard,
motivating the use of heuristic approaches. Their proposed solution consists of a greedy
placement algorithm followed by a local-search refinement step. The greedy algorithm
fills the array column by column. For each position, the eight neighboring features are
identified and the Hamming distance between the synthesis schedule of each candidate
barcode and those of the already assigned neighbors is computed. The barcode that
minimizes the summed Hamming distance to its neighbors is selected. To efficiently
evaluate all remaining barcode candidates at each step, GPU acceleration is employed.
Subsequently, a local-search procedure (2-OPT) iteratively swaps barcode pairs if the
exchange reduces the global layout cost.

\paragraph{Implementation and evaluation.}
In this project, the greedy algorithm followed by 2-OPT refinement was implemented
and adapted to our barcode design framework. Several modifications were introduced
to ensure consistent and comparable synthesis schedule representations.

First, a normalized synthesis encoding scheme was defined. In practice, naive synthesis
schedules may differ in length depending on nucleotide composition, preventing direct
Hamming distance comparison. To overcome this limitation, each barcode of length $N$
was encoded using a fixed-length binary vector of $4 \times N$ bits. For each nucleotide
position, a 4-bit one-hot representation was used (A, C, G, T), with exactly one bit set
to 1. For example:

\begin{verbatim}
ACTG  -> 1000 0100 0001 0010
AATG  -> 1000 1000 0001 0010
\end{verbatim}

This encoding ensures uniform schedule length and enables consistent Hamming distance
computation across all barcodes.

Second, layout quality was quantified by computing the mean Hamming distance between
each barcode and its eight immediate neighbors. This metric was evaluated for three
configurations: (i) a random layout (baseline), (ii) after greedy optimization, and
(iii) after greedy + 2-OPT refinement. This allowed direct measurement of cost
reduction achieved by each optimization stage.

Third, to assess whether similarity in synthesis schedules translates to sequence-level
similarity, we additionally computed the Levenshtein distance between neighboring
barcode sequences (rather than their synthesis encodings). This analysis provided
insight into the relationship between photolithographic schedule similarity and
biological sequence similarity.

Finally, spatial heatmaps were generated to visualize the mean Hamming distance of
each feature relative to its eight neighbors before and after optimization. These
visualizations revealed the emergence of local clusters of barcodes with reduced
schedule dissimilarity, consistent with the intended objective of minimizing
cross-feature synthesis discrepancies.

\paragraph{Co-design of barcodes and layout.}
In contrast to post-hoc layout optimization of pre-designed barcode libraries, we explored
alternative strategies that jointly design barcode sequences and spatial arrangements from
first principles. These co-design approaches explicitly embed spatial information into the
barcode structure itself, ensuring that sequence similarity correlates with physical proximity
on the array. Three distinct algorithmic frameworks were developed and evaluated in silico
across three array configurations: (i) 118$\times$182, (ii) full-density 235$\times$363,
and (iii) a 235$\times$363 checkerboard layout with 50\% occupancy (one barcode every
two positions), equivalent to two interleaved 118$\times$182 sub-arrays.

\textbf{Dual Barcodes (combinatorial clustering).}
This method partitions the array into regular spatial blocks (nominally $4 \times 4$ spots)
and encodes each position using two barcode components. The first component
(\textit{bar\_1}) serves as a block identifier shared by all spots in the same block. The
second component (\textit{bar\_2}) is assigned at spot level such that positions within
each block receive distinct \textit{bar\_2} values. Final spot identities are defined by
concatenation, $\textit{barcode}=\textit{bar\_1}+\textit{bar\_2}$, while enforcing global
uniqueness of the 36-nt combined sequence (18 nt + 18 nt). Barcode components are selected
from pre-existing libraries (not designed online during layout generation). For the
118$\times$182 generation, the input 18-nt libraries contained 21,476 candidates (each set)
. Boundary handling uses partial edge blocks when dimensions
are not divisible by four; the same assignment rules are applied to valid positions in
these truncated blocks (shared \textit{bar\_1}, unique \textit{bar\_2} within block). This
architecture provides two key properties: (i) all spots within a spatial block share the
same \textit{bar\_1} sequence, creating local clustering tolerant to synthesis errors
affecting the block-level identifier, and (ii) global barcode uniqueness is achieved with
fewer unique component sequences than total spots because uniqueness is carried by the pair
$(\textit{bar\_1},\textit{bar\_2})$. When evaluated through simulated error-prone
synthesis and sequencing (substitution rate 3\%, insertion rate 4\%, deletion rate 14\%),
this approach yielded a precision of 0.973 and recall of 0.570.

\textbf{Quadtree (hierarchical position encoding).}
Inspired by spatial indexing structures, this method encodes the (row, column) coordinate
of each spot through recursive subdivision of the array space. Each barcode position $l$
corresponds to one level of the hierarchy: the coordinate is scaled to a binary space of
$2^L$ bins, and at each cycle, the most significant unprocessed bit from both row and column
coordinates defines a quadrant index (0\textendash 3), which is mapped to a nucleotide (A, C, G, T).
To prevent synthesis errors arising from homopolymer runs, a run-length limiting (RLL)
algorithm is applied. For each cycle, a contingency table is constructed to count how many
spots at risk of violating the maximum run constraint would be affected by each of the 24
possible permutations of the quadrant-to-nucleotide mapping. The permutation that minimizes
violations is selected, and deterministic fallback rules enforce diversity when necessary.
This encoding intrinsically ensures that Hamming distance between barcodes scales
logarithmically with geometric distance on the array. Preliminary evaluation using a
36-nucleotide design (three rounds of 8 cycles plus 12 random nucleotides) achieved
precision of 0.899 and recall of 0.593.

\textbf{Zipcodes (Gray code spatial gradients).}
Gray codes are binary sequences in which successive integers differ by exactly one bit.
By independently applying Gray code encoding to row and column coordinates and translating
each 2-bit pair into a single nucleotide (00 $\rightarrow$ A, 01 $\rightarrow$ C,
10 $\rightarrow$ G, 11 $\rightarrow$ T), this method guarantees that spatially adjacent
spots differ by exactly one base in their position-encoding sequences. The final barcode
is constructed by concatenating the row-encoded segment, an optional inter-segment
separator (e.g., ``GGGG''), the column-encoded segment, and a random high-diversity
sequence for sequencing discrimination. This architecture provides the smoothest spatial
gradient among the tested methods: Hamming distance increases proportionally with Manhattan
distance along the array axes. Evaluation revealed that the choice of inter-segment separator
affects decoding performance. With a ``GGGG'' separator, precision was 0.830 and recall was
0.747 (the highest recall among all methods). Without a separator, precision improved to
0.901, while recall decreased to 0.604, illustrating a precision-recall tradeoff likely
mediated by the decoding threshold.

The implementation details of these co-design strategies are available in the
associated repository (\url{https://github.com/francops1722/Layout_opt}). 

\paragraph{Future perspectives.}

All barcode designs and layout optimization strategies described above were generated and
evaluated exclusively through computational simulation. Empirical validation through physical
array fabrication and sequencing remains an essential next step. Future work will involve
the photolithographic synthesis of arrays implementing each of the tested layout strategies\textemdash random
layout (baseline), greedy + 2-OPT optimization, Dual Barcodes, Quadtree, and
Zipcodes\textemdash followed by sequencing-based characterization under controlled conditions.
This will enable direct measurement of synthesis error rates, cross-feature contamination,
and decoding performance in real experimental settings. Such empirical data will be critical
for validating the predictive accuracy of the simulation framework and for identifying
conditions under which co-design strategies provide measurable improvements in array quality
and sequencing fidelity. Additionally, these experiments will inform refinements to both
algorithmic design and photolithographic synthesis protocols, ultimately guiding the
selection of optimal barcode-layout combinations for production-scale spatial transcriptomics
arrays.
